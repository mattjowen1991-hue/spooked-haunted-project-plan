What I'd take off your hands

You investigate, I edit

The idea is that you focus on the night itself and hand the footage over. For each investigation I would:

  • Sync all three cameras and cut between the angles so the action always has the best view
  • Cut the walking, waiting and setting up, so the episode moves from one moment to the next
  • Clean up the audio, and replay and caption anything you catch so viewers can hear it too
  • Build the history segment around your drone footage, with title cards
  • Cut the shorts from the same edit
  • Note chapter timestamps for each room, ready to paste in when you upload
  • Put the finished episode and shorts in the shared Drive, ready for you to download and upload

Turnaround target: the episode ready for your review within a week of the investigation.

The main episode

Every minute earns its place

This is the biggest change I'd suggest, so here's the research behind it rather than just my opinion.

What YouTube says

YouTube's own creator guidance says there's no ideal length, and tells creators to avoid filler and not stretch content out. Recommendations aren't based on raw minutes. YouTube measures what it calls valued watch time, which comes from viewers rating videos in satisfaction surveys, alongside likes and shares. An hour where people drift off or skip around counts for less than 40 minutes they enjoyed all the way through.

What the best in the genre do

Length on its own isn't the problem. Plenty of big channels run long. The difference is how hard they cut.

The pattern: TV cuts a whole night down to under an hour, and the UK channels a step or two ahead of you mostly sit between 40 and 50 minutes. When a night is quiet, the professionals cut harder or don't air it. When a night is huge, they split it into parts rather than one marathon.

What viewers say they want

In a 2023 survey of over 1,000 paranormal fans by Higgypop, 64% said over-dramatisation was their biggest frustration with ghost hunting shows, and 69% wanted more local folklore. People come for real moments and real history, not for the walk between rooms.

What I'd suggest

  • Aim for 35 to 50 minutes for a typical night. Let the night decide: a quiet one might be 30, a busy one 50.
  • The keep test. Every minute shows activity, history, a story beat or a genuine reaction. If it's walking, waiting or setting up, it goes.
  • Big nights become two-parters. That's two uploads from one investigation instead of one long one.
  • Chapters for each room so viewers can jump around without leaving the video.

A suggestion for trying it: make the next three episodes tighter, then compare them with the last three in YouTube Studio. Look at average view duration, how many people are still watching after 30 seconds, and likes per view. If the long format does better, we go back to it.

Showing your working builds trust. Viewers in this genre are quick to call things fake, so when they see what you checked along the way, the moments you call "that one was evidence beyond doubt" carry far more weight.

When to post

For a UK audience, evenings and weekends are the busy times, and the usual advice is to publish a couple of hours before your viewers are most active so YouTube has time to pick the video up. If you don't already have a set slot, I'd suggest Friday at around 6pm, at the same time every episode, so people learn when to expect it. The Audience tab in YouTube Studio shows when your own viewers are online, so we can fine-tune it from real data.

Comments and likes

Turning views into conversation

The episodes are clearly being watched. The next step is getting viewers to talk back. Comments and likes help YouTube decide what to recommend, and they're what turns a viewer into someone who comes back for every episode. A few small habits that I can build into each edit:

  • End on a question. Ask one specific thing near the end, like "what do you think the knocks in the cellar were?", and pin the same question as the first comment.
  • Ask after the first big moment. A quick like-and-subscribe nudge works better once viewers have been hooked than it does in the first minute.
  • Reply early. Answering comments in the first couple of hours after release gets more people joining in.
  • Use the Posts tab. Polls like "where should we investigate next?" are easy and give people a reason to come back between episodes.
  • Shorts that ask too. A short that ends on "real or not?" pulls in comments on its own.
Shorts

How many, and where

4 to 5 shorts per investigation, so 8 to 10 a month or roughly two a week. Shorts under 90 seconds hold on to far more of their viewers than longer ones, so I'd keep most of them between 20 and 60 seconds. One or two go out before the episode as teasers, the rest after, each pointing back to the full investigation.

Going live

Yes, but as a side dish

Live works well alongside the edited episode, as long as it doesn't replace it. My suggestion is one short live segment per investigation: a single room or vigil, 20 to 40 minutes, streamed from a phone while the three cameras keep recording as normal.

Why it helps

  • Viewers feel part of the night and can ask questions in chat, which is exactly the conversation the channel needs
  • At the end you can tell them the full episode drops on Friday, which builds an audience for release day
  • The live is raw and real-time. The episode is the polished story of the whole night with every angle, the history and the evidence review. They do different jobs.

How to do it

  • YouTube and Facebook: you're already well past the YouTube subscriber and Facebook follower minimums, so you can go live from the phone apps.
  • TikTok: usually needs around 1,000 followers there.
  • Turn live streaming on in YouTube Studio at least a day before the first time, because activation can take up to 24 hours.
  • Check the phone signal in the room first, as old buildings and cellars are often dead zones, and ask the venue if streaming is OK.

Filming with three cameras

Small habits that save hours

  • Two Canons moving, one GoPro still. CAM A and CAM B follow the team, ideally from different sides so they catch each other's reactions. The GoPro stays locked off covering the whole room, so if something happens off to one side, it's still caught. That mix of close-ups and a wide view is what makes the edit feel like a proper show.
  • Set the GoPro up first in every room. Point it at the spot most likely to see activity, like the trigger object or a doorway, and leave it alone.
  • Clap at the start of every vigil. One loud clap in view of all three cameras makes syncing almost instant.
  • Keep all three rolling for the whole vigil. Stopping and starting is where the moment always gets missed. If the cameras split long recordings into chunks, that's fine. The GoPro battery may not last a whole night, so check it between rooms.
  • Say it out loud. When something happens, someone calls out the time and what it was. It lands on the audio and helps me find it fast.
  • Grab the drone shots in daylight. A minute or two of the outside is all the history segment needs.

The incident log

The biggest time saver of all. Every episode comes from several hours of footage across three cameras, so a quick note of the moments that mattered goes a long way.

I've made a simple app for it. Tap Mark the moment something happens and the exact time is saved, then pick what it was and the room. It works with no signal, so it's fine in cellars, and at the end of the night you send the log to the WhatsApp group in one tap.
