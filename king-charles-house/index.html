Checklist

Optional extras

Talking to the staff

Not essential. The plan works without it. But if any of the staff are happy to chat, while it's quiet or as they close up, a few minutes with someone who works here every day can give you the best leads of the night.

Things to ask

  • Have you ever seen, heard or felt anything here? Where exactly, and roughly when?
  • Have you heard about the man in period dress? Has anyone you work with seen him?
  • Is there anywhere in the building staff don't like going on their own, especially at closing time?
  • Can the trap door be opened? Do you know anything about the skeleton story?
  • What natural noises should we know about? Fridges, pumps, pipes or the fire settling.

Getting it on camera

  • Only if they're happy to be filmed. Ask first, and ask if they're OK with their first name being used.
  • One or two minutes is plenty. The best time is after closing, while they're finishing up.
  • In the edit, it goes straight after the history piece, just like the witness interviews in the big shows.
  • If they'd rather not be filmed, add what they said as a note in the SHP Log, and say on camera "the staff told us..."

Anything they tell you can be used in the room cards and the question bank. A specific detail from someone who's experienced it makes the questions land better on camera.

Opening piece to camera

How to start

Film this outside, by the statue of Charles II slipping out of the house. Keep it under a minute. Say it in your own words. These are just prompts.

Prompt

"Tonight we're in Worcester, at the King Charles House. It was built in 1577, and in 1651 it was the headquarters of a king on the run. As the battle was lost, Charles II fled through this building with soldiers at the front door. Staff say something is still here. Tonight we find out."

  • Introduce who's here tonight in one line each
  • Say what you're hoping to find, ideally one specific thing
  • Film a few seconds of the outside and the street. It's the shot that opens the history segment.
The history piece

What to say, and when

Film this early, before the vigils, standing by the carved fireplace in the bar. In the edit it goes right after the cold open and title, so viewers know why the place matters before anything happens. Two to three minutes is plenty. Here's the story in the order to tell it.

1 · The house

Built in 1577, and one local history says it was built by a wealthy Worcester brewer, Richard Durant. A carved inscription on the building has the date and the words "Love God, honour ye King". Other accounts say it belonged to the Berkeley family. Even local historians disagree about who owned it.

2 · The battle

3 September 1651, the Battle of Worcester. Around 16,000 men under the young Charles II against roughly 28,000 under Oliver Cromwell. When Parliament's troops took Fort Royal, just outside the city, and turned the guns on Worcester, it was over.

3 · The escape

Charles used this house as his headquarters. As Cromwell's men broke into the city, he ran back here. Tradition says that as the soldiers reached the front door, he slipped out of the back, got on a horse and rode out through St Martin's Gate. A last cavalry charge down the street bought him the time to get away.

4 · The cost

About 3,000 Royalist soldiers died that day, and around 10,000 were taken prisoner. Thousands of Scottish prisoners were later shipped to the American colonies and the Caribbean as forced labour. It was the last major battle of the English Civil War.

5 · What happened next

Charles spent six weeks on the run with a £1,000 price on his head. Three days after the battle he hid all day in an oak tree at Boscobel while soldiers searched the woods below. That's the Royal Oak in all those pub names. He finally escaped to France in October.

Save the oubliette and the carvings for their own rooms. They work better as surprises on the walk round than in the history piece.

Room by room

What to say in each room

Each card has what to say on camera when you walk in, what's been reported, and something specific to try. Confirm which rooms you can use on the night, and use the blank card at the end for anywhere else.

Room 1 · Way in

The front door, New Street

Say on camera

"This is the door Charles II ran in through. As Cromwell's men came down New Street, locals are said to have pushed carts and oxen into the road to slow them down."

Try this

Stand outside and walk the route he would have taken through the building to the back door. It's a great shot for the history segment, and it shows viewers the layout before you go dark.

Room 2 · The heart of the house

The bar and the carved fireplace

Say on camera

"Look at this carving over the fireplace. It's from the 1600s, and it's full of the devil. Here he's coming in through a window and scaring a group of card players, and one of them is hiding under the table. Here he's raising a pint to men drinking. Nobody's sure what it means. It might be a warning against gambling, or a comment on the Civil War."

Good to know

The carving is thought to have been brought here from another house of the same age that was demolished. Look for the three green men at the top.

Try this

A trigger object tied to the carving: lay a few playing cards face down on a table in front of the fireplace, mark their position, and ask whoever's here to move one. Lock CAM A on the table.

Watch out for

BaselineThe real fire ticks and settles as it cools, and the bar has fridges and pumps. Call those noises out when they happen.

Room 3 · Under the floor

The trap door and the oubliette

Say on camera

"This trap door at the back of the pub isn't a cellar. It's an oubliette, from the French for 'to forget'. It's a bottle-shaped hole where the hatch is the only way in or out. In the 1700s a magistrate is said to have used it to hold the accused overnight before they went to court."

Good to know

One pub listing claims there's a skeleton in it. Treat that as a legend unless the staff can tell you more, and say so on camera if you mention it. The building was also used as judges' quarters at one point, which fits the magistrate story.

Try this

An EVP session sitting around the hatch, with a recorder placed right on it, or inside if the staff allow and it's safe. Ask questions about being held and being forgotten. Only open it if the venue says yes.

Room 4 · The escape route

The back door

Say on camera

"This is where Charles II got out. With soldiers at the front, he left through here, got on a horse and rode for St Martin's Gate. Accounts say the cavalry charge that covered him was led by the Earl of Cleveland and a Major Careless. Three days later, Careless was the man hiding up the oak tree with him."

Try this

Recreate the moment. One person stands at the back door, the rest at the front, and you call out to Charles directly. "We know you came through here. Were you afraid?" Keep both cameras on the doorway.

Room 5 · Upstairs

The first floor and the stairs

Say on camera

"These stairs are steep and winding, and staff have heard footsteps in this building over the years. In 1651 the king used this house as his headquarters, so somewhere in this building he and his officers planned their last stand."

Good to know

There's a piano up here for visiting musicians. The stairs are narrow and steep, so go slowly in the dark and keep the torches on until everyone's settled.

Try this

Use the piano as a trigger object. Lock CAM A on the keys, close the lid if they'll let you, and ask for one note. Then a footsteps test: everyone sits still in silence for two minutes and listens to the stairs.

Room 6 · If you can get in

The cellar

Good to know

Nothing specific is recorded about the cellar. If you get the chance to ask the staff, find out whether they've had experiences down there. If they have, it becomes a strong room. If not, use it for a short, quiet lights-out session.

Watch out for

BaselineBeer lines, cooling equipment and drains make plenty of noise. Do a full baseline before you start asking questions.

Every room, every time

How to run a vigil

The same steps in every room make the night easier to film and much faster to edit.

  1. Clap and log itOne loud clap in view of both cameras, then tap Vigil start / clap in the SHP Log.
  2. Say where you areRoom name and time out loud, plus who's in the room.
  3. Two minutes of silenceLights off, nobody talks. You hear what the room sounds like on its own, and it's often when things happen.
  4. Introduce yourselvesTalk to whoever might be there, calmly and politely. Explain why you've come.
  5. Questions with long pausesAsk one at a time and leave a good 10 to 15 seconds after each one. Viewers need the silence to hear any answer.
  6. Call out your own noises"That was my stomach." "That was a car outside." Saying it out loud saves hours in the edit and makes the real moments believable.
  7. Mark anything that happensTap Mark in the SHP Log straight away, then say out loud what you heard or saw.
  8. Sum up before you leaveLights on and 30 seconds to camera: what happened in here, and how did it feel? These make the best shorts.
Question bank

What to ask here

Questions about this building's real history land better on camera than general ones. Mix them with yes or no questions so any knocks or device reactions have something to answer.

The last half hour

How to wind down

  1. Final vigilEveryone back in the room that was most active. A short session and one last direct question.
  2. Lights on and say goodbyeTell whoever's there you're leaving and thank them. It's a nice moment on camera and it's respectful.
  3. The verdictThe closing piece to camera, outside if you can. Each person answers one question: what was the moment of the night for you? Then your overall take, honestly.
  4. Thank the venue on cameraName the pub, say it's open to visit, and thank the staff. Venues share these, and it gets you invited back.
  5. Tease the next locationOne line about where you're going next.
  6. Send the logs and pack downEveryone taps Send log in the SHP Log. Check every room for kit before you leave, and stop the cameras last.
Closing prompt

"The King Charles House was a king's last stop before six weeks on the run. Tonight we heard [the moment]. Was it him, a prisoner from under the floor, or something else? Tell us in the comments what you think."
